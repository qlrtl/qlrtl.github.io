About/əˈbaʊt/

I am a graduate student in linguistics working at the interface of pragmatics and sociolinguistics. My work asks how social meaning is made in the gap between what is said and how it is said. Why does a speaker choose a roundabout phrasing over a plain one, or a formal register over a casual one, and how do listeners read those choices as signs of stance, persona, and relationship?

I hold a B.A. in Linguistics from Stony Brook University, where I worked as a teaching assistant and research assistant, and I studied linguistics at Korea University in Seoul. In 2026 I completed the Michigan Humanities Emerging Research Scholars Program (MICHHERS) at the University of Michigan’s Rackham Graduate School.

When the proposition holds still and only the wording moves, what exactly has changed?

Intralingual translation and social meaning

When speakers rephrase something within the same language, moving up or down in register or talking around a word instead of using it, the propositional content can stay nearly constant while the social content shifts sharply. My thesis treats these rewordings as a natural experiment. Working within third-wave variationist sociolinguistics and theories of indexicality, I examine how register shift and circumlocution come to signal interpersonal and social qualities such as politeness, intimacy, authority, and evasiveness.

More broadly, I am interested in how pragmatic inference and indexical meaning interact: how speakers draw on shared expectations about what is normally said, and how departures from those expectations become resources for constructing identity.
